Nurses are trained to notice when something is wrong with someone else. But when it comes to your own mental health, it can be easy to minimize, push through, or tell yourself you should be able to handle it.

You are allowed to need support. You are allowed to be tired. You are allowed to take your mental health seriously, even if you are the person everyone else depends on.

If you are in immediate danger or may hurt yourself or someone else, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room. If you need emotional crisis support in the United States, call or text 988 or chat at 988lifeline.org.

This post is for general education and support. It is not a diagnosis, treatment plan, or substitute for professional mental healthcare.

This guide is the serious-support page in the Nursing Life wellness group. For lighter post-shift routines, read Self-Care Ideas After a 12-Hour Shift. For everyday stress tools, read Stress Management for Nurses.

1. Signs Your Mental Health May Need Attention

Everyone has hard days, especially in healthcare. But when symptoms last, intensify, or interfere with daily life, it is worth paying attention.

  • You feel anxious, numb, angry, or tearful more often than usual.
  • You are sleeping too much, sleeping too little, or waking up exhausted.
  • You are isolating from people you normally care about.
  • You feel detached from patients, coworkers, family, or yourself.
  • You are using alcohol, food, shopping, scrolling, or overtime to avoid feelings.
  • You have lost interest in things that usually help you feel like yourself.
  • You feel hopeless, trapped, or like people would be better off without you.

2. Start With One Honest Check-In

You do not have to solve everything today. Start by telling the truth about where you are.

Ask yourself:

  • What has felt heavy lately?
  • What am I pretending is fine?
  • What do I need more of: sleep, help, connection, time off, therapy, boundaries?
  • Who is one safe person I can tell?

3. Use Support Before You Hit Empty

You do not need to wait until things are unbearable to get help. Support can look different depending on what you need and what is available.

  • Primary care provider
  • Therapist or counselor
  • Psychiatry provider
  • Employee Assistance Program
  • Peer support group
  • Trusted friend, mentor, or family member
  • 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline for urgent emotional support

4. Create a Small Mental Health Care Plan

A care plan does not have to be complicated. It just needs to make the next right step easier when you are tired.

  • One person to contact: someone you can text when you are struggling.
  • One professional resource: a provider, EAP number, therapy platform, or local clinic.
  • One grounding habit: breathing, walking, journaling, stretching, prayer, meditation, or quiet time.
  • One boundary: a shift, request, or commitment you need to stop automatically saying yes to.
  • One emergency option: 988, 911, or your nearest emergency department if safety becomes urgent.

5. Make Recovery Feel Practical

Mental health support is not only therapy appointments. It is also the daily structure that makes life feel survivable again.

  • Eat something with protein before or after your shift.
  • Get outside for a few minutes of daylight when possible.
  • Move your body gently, even if it is only stretching.
  • Reduce one avoidable source of stress this week.
  • Plan one thing that is not about work.

What Support Can Look Like

Support does not have to wait until a crisis. It may be a primary care appointment, a therapy consult, an EAP call, a medication discussion with a qualified clinician, a trusted mentor, a peer support group, or a conversation with someone who will not dismiss what you are feeling.
